---
name: reduce-the-work
description: "Cut the decisions, steps and interruptions a task costs its user."
---

Count the task's real cost before improving its appearance: the decisions the
person must make, the steps they must take, the times they are interrupted to
supply something, and the times they must go somewhere else to find out what
happened. Reducing that count is design work, not a follow-up.

For each decision, ask whether the system already knows the answer, can
reasonably default it, or can defer it until it matters; remove the ones that
survive none of those. For each step, ask what it exists to protect and whether
a safe path can carry it. For each interruption, ask whether the work could
proceed and disclose, rather than stop and ask.

This covers the whole task, not one screen: command-line output, first-run
setup, permission and approval prompts, waiting states and the recovery path
after a failure are part of the same journey. A failure that leaves the person
to reconstruct where they were is a design defect.

Truthful state is the constraint, never the excuse. Accurately displaying an
avoidable complication is a worse outcome than removing it; inventing a
comfortable default that hides real state is worse than both. When a decision
genuinely must be the person's, make it small, informed and reversible, and
say what happens if they do nothing.
